The collinear points sit at fixed fractions of the Earth–Moon separation, and that separation runs from 356 500 km at perigee to 406 700 km at apogee. So they breathe in and out every month: L1 alone moves about 42 600 km, L2 about 58 600 km. A libration point is not a place you could paint a marker on.

What these numbers are, and are not

The Moon's position

From the truncated ELP-2000/82B series — the standard 60-term longitude and radius tables with the 60-term latitude table, in lunar.js. Not a circle and not a fixed ellipse: the evection term alone swings the Moon ±1.27° and the variation another ±0.66°, so anything simpler is wrong by degrees.

Checked against JPL Horizons over 731 daily samples across two years: median angular error 1.9″, worst 9.5″, and distance within 27 km typical, 45 km worst. For scale the Moon's disc is 1865″ across, so the worst case is about 1/200 of a lunar diameter.

A libration point is a rotating-frame idea

The five points are the stationary points of the effective potential in the frame that turns with the Moon — where gravity and the centrifugal term cancel. In the inertial frame drawn here they are not stationary at all; they sweep round the Earth once a month, which is exactly what the view shows.

They are also solutions of the circular restricted three-body problem. The real lunar orbit has e = 0.055, so the points pulse with the month. Treating them as fixed is the first approximation, not the truth.

Solved, not approximated

L1, L2 and L3 are roots of a quintic and are found here by bracketed bisection to machine precision — residual gradient at each point is ~10⁻¹⁶.

The familiar shortcut R(μ/3)^⅓, the Hill radius, puts L1 and L2 at 61 279 km from the Moon. The true roots are 58 024 km and 64 521 km, so that shortcut is wrong by about 3 250 km in opposite directions. It is a decent mnemonic and a poor answer.

Not the ones JWST uses

These are the Earth–Moon points, a few hundred thousand kilometres away. JWST sits at Sun–Earth L2 and SOHO at Sun–Earth L1, each about 1.50 million km from Earth — roughly four times further out than the Moon. Same mathematics, different pair of bodies, and the two get confused constantly.

That confusion has a measurable victim. The instability timescale quoted almost everywhere for libration points is 23 days — and that is the Sun–Earth figure. Run the same linearisation on the Earth–Moon pair and L1 e-folds in 1.5 days, L2 in 2.0. Twelve times faster, which is why ARTEMIS station-kept about weekly rather than monthly.

Nothing sits at a collinear point in any case. L1, L2 and L3 are saddles — stable along one axis, unstable along another — so anything placed there slides off. Real missions fly halo or Lissajous orbits around the point: Queqiao-1 has held a halo about EML2 since 2018 to relay for the lunar far side, which never sees Earth at all.

The Moon does not orbit the Earth's centre

Both bodies orbit their common barycentre, which lies — from Earth's centre. The Earth's radius is 6371 km, so the barycentre is inside the Earth — the Earth wobbles about an interior point rather than swinging around an external one. It is marked in the view.

The Sun is not a small correction

The Sun pulls on the Moon 2.20 times harder than the Earth does: 5.93×10⁻⁶ against 2.70×10⁻⁶ km/s². One consequence, easy to check and widely disbelieved — the Moon's path around the Sun is always concave toward the Sun. It never loops backwards.

So the three-body picture is itself an idealisation, and it matters most at L4 and L5. The textbook test is the Routh criterion: triangular points are stable when μ < 0.0385, and Earth–Moon's 0.0122 passes comfortably. But that is the verdict for an isolated pair. Add the Sun and the strict equilibrium does not survive — what remains near L4/L5 are a few periodic substitute orbits rather than a stable point, and test particles started there wander chaotically, many escaping.

Which is the honest reason the table says “in theory”, and why the Kordylewski dust clouds reported near L4/L5 since 1961 remain unconfirmed rather than established.
