What these numbers are, and are not

There are no lunar TLEs

The Earth console runs on SGP4, which exists only for Earth satellites described by two-line elements. Celestrak's own catalogue record for LRO settles it: DATA_STATUS_CODE = NEA — No Elements Available — with ORBIT_CENTER = MO and every orbital field blank. Asking for its elements returns No GP data found.

So this page cannot reuse that half of the program. It carries daily osculating element sets baked from JPL Horizons instead, and propagates between them.

Why daily, and not one element set

The Moon's gravity field is dominated by mascons rather than by a smooth J2 term, so the quantities a Keplerian propagator holds constant do not stay constant. Measured from Horizons: LRO's argument of periapsis moves about 3.1°/day and its period grows about 1.7 s every four hours. Period error integrates into along-track error, so a single element set puts the spacecraft on the wrong side of the Moon within weeks.

Adding J2 does not rescue that — it captures nodal regression and misses the part that hurts. A fresh anchor does. Checked against Horizons' own sub-observer point over 289 samples, in UTC: 1.4 km within two hours of an anchor, 20 km at ten to twelve, against 32–57 km for one element set held for days. Chandrayaan-2's orbit loosens faster, 35 km at ten to twelve, so each craft carries its own curve. The panel shows which regime you are in.

That is agreement with Horizons, not with the spacecraft. Past the last tracking data in its record, Horizons is predicting as well, and its error then comes on top and is not published. Two Horizons revisions of Chandrayaan-2 were 104 km apart twelve days after the older one was issued; Danuri's, 21 km after thirteen. Every anchor on this page that falls in a prediction is marked tracked · predicted, and for LRO that is all of them.

The rotation is the hard part

Earth's GMST is a smooth polynomial. The Moon's orientation is a polynomial plus a 13-term libration series applied to the pole's right ascension and declination and to the prime meridian. It is not a refinement: leave it out and the sub-spacecraft point is wrong by 44 km. With it, 0.17 km against Horizons over 289 epochs.

One trap worth recording — the NAIF series rates are per Julian century while every other term in the model is per day. Reading them as daily rates advances the arguments 36 525× too fast and puts the pole 1.9° out.

Three grades of knowing

tracked — daily element sets from JPL Horizons, whose trajectories are fit to the agencies' own tracking data up to a date the record states, and predicted after it. LRO, Chandrayaan-2 and Danuri.

published — no ephemeris exists publicly; the orbit is rebuilt from published mission parameters. Right size and shape, but the phase — where along the orbit it actually is — is not knowable.

schematic — the orbit family is known and little else. A halo orbit is not a conic and cannot be drawn from elements at all, so it is marked rather than propagated.

Why the Chinese craft are here

Horizons carries no Chinese lunar spacecraft. A name search for Queqiao returns "No matches found", and Chang* matches only three spent boosters. CNSA publishes no machine-readable ephemeris, and this is why tracking sites that draw from Horizons show an empty Moon where those spacecraft are.

The choice is to show them honestly labelled or leave them off. Leaving them off asserts that three spacecraft are at the Moon, which is false. Showing them as though tracked would be worse than both. So they are here, with the grade stated on every row.

The far side is the whole story

From a far-side site the Earth never rises — elevation is permanently negative, not merely low. Chang'e-4 and Chang'e-6 both landed there, and neither could have returned a single bit directly. That is the entire reason Queqiao exists.

The surface table computes Earth elevation for every site, so the ones that can never phone home are visible rather than asserted.

A lunar ground track is nearly a great circle

The Moon's surface rotates beneath an orbiter at only about 4.6 m/s, against roughly 1.56 km/s of orbital ground speed — 0.3 %, where a LEO satellite sees about 6 %. So successive passes barely shift: LRO's equator crossings move west by only 1.07°, about 32 km, per revolution.

That is why the track here looks like a tilted circle rather than the sheared sine wave the Earth page draws, and why global coverage takes a month rather than a day.

Frames, and why they agree

Landing-site coordinates are published in the mean Earth / polar axis (ME) frame, the cartographic standard. The IAU/WGCCRE series implemented here is an approximation to that same frame — pck00011.tpc describes it as one — valid to about 150 m (WGCCRE, Archinal et al. 2011). So sites and rotation share a frame, and the 0.17 km agreement with Horizons fits that.

The other lunar frame, principal axis (PA), is where the Moon's inertia tensor is diagonal and the one ephemerides integrate in. It differs from ME by about 0.029°, roughly 875 m on the surface (NAIF lunar frame kernels). This page does not use it, and an earlier note here that said the IAU series was closer to PA had it backwards.

Orbital elements are a third question. The rail and the table give i, Ω and ω against the lunar equator of date, the IAU pole; the baked sets are in ICRF, the Earth's equator, where the same orbits read 5–21° differently.
